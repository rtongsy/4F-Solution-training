4F Solution — US Lesson 1: Reading the need

Before any product — what is actually at risk?

A product recommendation without a defined need is a guess dressed as advice. Four questions must be answered before any product conversation begins.

Question 1 — Income
What income would stop — and for how long does the plan need it replaced?
A 32-year-old with a 30-year mortgage and two young children needs income replaced for a fundamentally different period than a 58-year-old whose mortgage is paid. The duration shapes the product.
Question 2 — Obligations
What survives regardless of what happens to the client?
The mortgage still comes due. Children still need to be fed and educated. Debt still accrues. Student loans, car payments, and medical obligations persist. List every obligation the plan depends on the client funding — and what happens to each one if they cannot. That list is the gap the protection plan exists to close.
Question 3 — Existing coverage
What does it actually provide — not what does it say on paper?
Group life at 2x salary sounds like coverage until you put it next to a $500,000 mortgage. Group STD/LTD at 60% sounds adequate until you calculate the after-tax benefit, note that it excludes bonuses, and find the 90-day elimination period. The professional reads the actual policy — not the benefits summary card.
Question 4 — The gap
Expressed in specific dollars, for each risk event.
Not "they need more coverage." The gap is: if this client dies tomorrow, their family faces a $620,000 shortfall. If they become disabled, their monthly income drops by $2,100 against obligations of $4,800. Each number connects to a specific product conversation. Vague gaps produce vague recommendations.
The discipline
Do not open a product conversation until all four questions have answers. A professional who can say "here is exactly what you are exposed to, and here is exactly what closes each gap" is operating at a different level than one who presents a menu of products and monthly costs.

4F Solution — US Lesson 3: Group benefits — reading them correctly

Group benefits are not a plan — they are a starting point

Most American employees with group benefits believe they are covered. Few have read what they actually have. The professional's job is to do that reading and surface the gaps in the client's own numbers.

Group life — the math problem
Employer group life at 1–2x salary is common. On a $95,000 salary that is $95,000–$190,000 of coverage. Put that next to a $450,000 mortgage, $40,000 in student loans, $35,000 in consumer debt, two children who need to be raised and educated, and a spouse who earns less. The gap is not abstract. Calculate it. The client cannot see the problem until someone runs the numbers.
Group STD/LTD — three questions to ask
1. Who pays the premium? If the employer pays the LTD premium, the benefit is taxable when received — 60% of gross income becomes significantly less after tax. If the employee pays with after-tax dollars, the benefit is tax-free. This changes the effective replacement rate materially.
2. What is the definition of disability? "Own occupation" (can't do your specific job) vs. "any occupation" (can't do any job) — a massive difference for professionals. Own occupation is the standard professionals need; many group plans default to "any occupation" after 24 months.
3. What is excluded? Pre-existing conditions, mental health limitations, and self-reported conditions frequently carry exclusions or caps in group plans.
Portability — the job-change risk
Group coverage does not follow the client. A 44-year-old employee who leaves their employer loses their group life and disability coverage — and may now face re-underwriting on individual policies at a significantly higher rate or with exclusions for health conditions that developed since their group coverage began. The professional who raises this question is the one who creates insurance independently of employment status.
The discovery question for group benefits
"What coverage does your employer provide — and have you ever read the actual policy to understand what it would pay and when? And would your coverage change if you left this job tomorrow?"

4F Solution — US Lesson 5: Priority and sequencing

When cash flow cannot close every gap at once

Three variables determine priority. Every gap is assessed against all three — the combination determines where it sits in the sequence.

⚡
Severity
What is the consequence if this gap is not closed?
📊
Probability
How likely is this event for this specific client?
💰
Cost to close
What does addressing this gap require relative to cash flow?
Priority framework — income protection first
Income is the engine of the entire plan. If it stops, every other Fundamental fails. For a working-age client with obligations, disability insurance is almost always the highest-priority gap. Life insurance comes next when there are dependants. Critical illness ranks above LTC for clients under 55. LTC planning belongs in the discussion for clients approaching or in their late 40s.
Managing cost without sacrificing core coverage
Extend the elimination period — a 90-day wait instead of 30 days meaningfully reduces the disability premium. The emergency reserve funds the gap.
Use term over permanent — for temporary obligations, term provides full coverage at a fraction of the cost, freeing cash flow for other gaps.
Layer coverage — start with the most catastrophic gap (death or total disability). Add CI and LTC as cash flow improves.
Leverage employer coverage wisely — group coverage is not adequate alone, but combining it with an individual policy to top up to the right amount reduces individual premium.
Connecting protection back to values — always
Every protection recommendation connects to something the client said matters. "You told me your family's financial security is everything to you. This recommendation exists because of that." The product is the mechanism. The value is the reason.
4F Solution — US summary review

The US protection picture — complete

Scan before the knowledge check.

Four questions first
Income at risk · Obligations that survive · Existing coverage (actual, not summary) · Gap in specific dollars. No product conversation opens without all four.
Product logic
Term (temporary) · Permanent (ongoing) · Individual DI (income) · CI (lump-sum health event) · LTC/hybrid (care costs). Need defines product — always.
Group benefits reality
1–2x salary life ≠ adequate. LTD taxation depends on who pays the premium. Own-occ vs. any-occ changes the real benefit. Coverage does not follow the client when they change employers.
Priority when constrained
Income first (DI) · Life (dependants) · CI · LTC. Severity × Probability × Cost to close = sequence. Extend elimination period to control DI cost. Use term for temporary obligations.
The key US distinction
In the US, health insurance itself is a protection gap for many clients — especially the self-employed, those between jobs, or those with high-deductible plans. The CI conversation is not just about cancer and stroke — it is also about the $5,000–$10,000 deductible and out-of-pocket maximum that sits underneath even good health insurance. CI funds the cost health insurance does not cover.
