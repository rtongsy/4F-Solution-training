Tax Efficiency — US Track

Tax efficiency is one of the most powerful tools in your client's financial plan — yet it's often the least understood. This module explores how to legally minimize tax at every stage of a client's financial life.

Three Tax Stages:
Every dollar a client earns passes through up to three tax events — when it's earned and saved, when it's accessed and spent, and when it's transferred to heirs. A skilled professional designs the plan to minimize taxation at each stage.

Lesson 1: The Three Tax Stages

Tax planning isn't a single decision — it's a system designed across three distinct stages of a client's financial life.

Stage 1: Accumulation

Taxes paid (or deferred) as income is earned and saved.

  • Pre-tax contributions (Traditional 401(k)/IRA) defer tax
  • After-tax contributions (Roth) pay tax now, grow tax-free
  • Non-deductible contributions: taxable growth

Stage 2: Distribution

Taxes paid when funds are accessed.

  • Traditional account withdrawals: fully taxable as ordinary income
  • Roth withdrawals (qualified): tax-free
  • Capital gains: taxed at preferential rates
  • RMDs force taxable income after age 73

Stage 3: Transfer

Tax implications at death or transfer to heirs.

  • Step-up in basis: inherited assets receive a new FMV cost basis — unrealized capital gains are eliminated
  • Federal estate tax: applies above $13.61M exemption (2024, indexed)
  • Life insurance death benefit: paid income-tax-free to beneficiaries
  • Inherited IRAs: generally must be distributed within 10 years (non-spouse)
The professional's job is to design a plan that minimizes total tax across all three stages — not just the current year.

Lesson 3: Traditional 401(k) and IRA

Traditional pre-tax accounts are the foundation of most US retirement plans. Contributions reduce taxable income today, but all withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

2025 Contribution Limits
  • 401(k) / 403(b) employee deferral: $23,500
  • Catch-up (age 50+): additional $7,500 → $31,000 total
  • Super catch-up (age 60–63, SECURE 2.0): additional $11,250 → $34,750 total
  • Traditional IRA: $7,000 ($8,000 age 50+)
  • IRA deductibility: phases out if covered by a workplace plan at certain income levels
Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)
  • RMDs begin at age 73 (SECURE 2.0 Act)
  • RMD amount = prior Dec 31 balance ÷ IRS life expectancy factor
  • Failure to take RMD: 25% excise tax on the shortfall (reduced to 10% if corrected timely)
  • Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime
Spousal IRA

A non-working spouse can contribute to a Traditional IRA based on the working spouse's earned income. No attribution rules apply — the spousal IRA is owned solely by the non-working spouse.

Large pre-tax balances create a future tax liability. Clients who accumulate significant Traditional IRA / 401(k) assets may face substantial RMD income and potential Medicare IRMAA surcharges.

Lesson 4: Roth IRA

The Roth IRA is one of the most powerful tax-planning tools in the US. Contributions are made with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals — including all growth — are completely tax-free.

2025 Contribution Limits and Income Eligibility
  • Contribution limit: $7,000 ($8,000 age 50+)
  • Phase-out begins (2024): $146,000 single / $230,000 married filing jointly
  • Phase-out complete: $161,000 single / $240,000 MFJ
  • Backdoor Roth: high earners make a non-deductible Traditional IRA contribution, then convert to Roth — legal under current law
Key Advantages
  • No RMDs during the owner's lifetime
  • Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn any time without tax or penalty
  • Qualified distributions don't affect Medicare IRMAA thresholds
  • First-time home purchase: up to $10,000 lifetime penalty-free withdrawal of earnings
  • Inherited Roth IRA: non-spouse beneficiaries must fully distribute within 10 years, but withdrawals remain income-tax-free
Roth 401(k)

Many employer plans now offer a Roth 401(k) option — same contribution limits as Traditional 401(k) but after-tax. No income limits apply. Subject to RMDs (but can be rolled to Roth IRA to avoid this).

Roth conversions: Clients with Traditional IRA/401(k) balances can convert amounts to Roth in low-income years — paying tax now to eliminate future RMDs and create tax-free income. This is the US equivalent of RRSP meltdown planning.

Lesson 5: Traditional vs. Roth — When Each Wins

The core question: will your client's tax rate be higher now or in retirement? The right answer determines which account type wins.

Client SituationFavorsReason
High income, peak earning yearsTraditionalDeduction at high rate now; withdrawals in lower bracket later
Early career, low incomeRothPay tax at low rate now; tax-free growth for decades
Expects higher future tax ratesRothLock in today's rates
Large pre-tax balance, approaching 73Roth conversionReduce future RMD burden; manage IRMAA thresholds
Wants to pass wealth tax-efficientlyRothNo RMDs, tax-free to heirs
Managing Medicare IRMAARoth / brokerageQualified Roth withdrawals don't count as MAGI
Tax diversification is often the best answer. Clients with both Traditional and Roth accounts have flexibility to manage taxable income in retirement — drawing from whichever account keeps them in the most favorable bracket each year.
The Break-Even Analysis

For clients in the same marginal rate now vs. retirement: Roth and Traditional are mathematically equivalent. The Roth wins if rates rise; Traditional wins if rates fall. When uncertain, split contributions between both.

Lesson 6: Purpose-Built Tax-Advantaged Accounts

Beyond retirement accounts, the US tax code provides specialized vehicles for healthcare, education, and disability planning — each with unique advantages.

HSA — Health Savings Account
  • Available only with a High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)
  • 2025 limits: $4,300 individual / $8,550 family
  • Triple tax advantage: contributions deductible → growth tax-free → withdrawals tax-free for qualified medical expenses
  • After age 65: withdrawals for any purpose taxed as ordinary income (like Traditional IRA) — medical withdrawals remain tax-free
  • No RMDs; rolls over year to year; powerful long-term healthcare reserve
529 Plan — Education Savings
  • After-tax contributions; tax-free growth and withdrawal for qualified education expenses
  • Many states offer a state income tax deduction for contributions
  • No federal contribution limit, but subject to gift tax annual exclusion ($18,000/yr per beneficiary in 2024)
  • 5-year superfunding: lump-sum $90,000 and elect to spread over 5 years for gift tax purposes
  • SECURE 2.0: up to $35,000 lifetime can be rolled to Roth IRA (after 15 years, annual IRA limits apply)
  • Unused funds: change beneficiary to another family member
ABLE Account — Disability Savings
  • For individuals with disabilities diagnosed before age 26
  • 2025 contribution limit: $18,000/year
  • After-tax contributions; tax-free growth and withdrawals for qualified disability expenses
  • Does not affect most means-tested benefit eligibility (SSI, Medicaid)
No US equivalent to Canada's FHSA. However, Roth IRA has a first-home exception ($10K lifetime penalty-free withdrawal of earnings). Some states offer first-home savings programs.

Lesson 7: Account Sequencing — Where to Put the Next Dollar

When a client has limited savings dollars, the order of contribution matters. This hierarchy maximizes available tax advantages before moving to less-efficient vehicles.

Recommended Sequencing (general guidance — always personalize):
  1. Employer 401(k) up to full match — free money; always capture first
  2. HSA to maximum (if HDHP eligible) — triple tax advantage; best tax-efficiency per dollar
  3. Roth IRA to maximum ($7,000) — after-tax now, tax-free forever; no RMDs
  4. Maximize 401(k) / 403(b) contributions — $23,500 total (or Traditional, depending on bracket)
  5. 529 contributions (if education goal exists)
  6. Taxable brokerage — most flexible, least tax-efficient; last resort
Exceptions and Adjustments
  • High earners above Roth income limits: use Backdoor Roth before maxing Traditional 401(k)
  • Business owners: add SEP-IRA ($70,000 limit) or Solo 401(k) before taxable accounts
  • Near retirement and large pre-tax balance: Roth conversions may outperform additional Traditional contributions
  • Debt with high interest: pay off before steps 3–6 (employer match always comes first)
The employer match is non-negotiable. A 50% match = instant 50% return. No investment can reliably beat it. Always ensure the client captures the full match before anything else.

The professional's role is to understand the client's full picture — income, goals, timeline, risk — and sequence savings appropriately.

Lesson 8: Permanent Life Insurance Across All Three Stages

Properly structured permanent life insurance can play a role in all three tax stages — making it one of the most versatile tools in advanced tax planning.

Stage 1: Accumulation — Tax-Deferred Growth
  • Cash value inside a permanent policy grows tax-deferred
  • No annual contribution limits (unlike qualified plans)
  • MEC (Modified Endowment Contract) rules: over-funded policies lose tax-favored loan/withdrawal treatment — stay within IRS 7-pay test limits
  • Ideal for high earners who have maximized all qualified accounts
Stage 2: Distribution — Tax-Advantaged Income (LIRP Strategy)
  • Policy loans are not taxable income — funds accessed as loans against cash value
  • Life Insurance Retirement Plan (LIRP): accumulate in IUL/whole life, access via loans in retirement
  • Loan income does not count toward Medicare IRMAA thresholds
  • Does not affect Social Security taxation calculations
  • Policy must remain in force — policy lapse with outstanding loans triggers a taxable event
Stage 3: Transfer — Tax-Free Death Benefit
  • Death benefit paid income-tax-free to named beneficiaries under IRC §101(a)
  • Passes outside the probate estate (directly to named beneficiaries)
  • Can fund estate tax liability or equalize inheritance between heirs
  • Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT): removes death benefit from taxable estate for large estates
For clients who have maximized 401(k), Roth IRA, and HSA, a well-funded permanent life policy is often the next most tax-efficient vehicle available.

Lesson 9: Distribution Planning

The distribution stage — when clients begin drawing on accumulated assets — is where much of the value of tax planning is realized or lost.

RMDs and Bracket Management
  • RMDs begin at age 73 (SECURE 2.0); Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs
  • Large pre-tax balances create large forced RMDs — which can push clients into higher brackets
  • Roth conversion ladder: convert Traditional IRA to Roth in lower-income years (early retirement, before RMD age) to reduce future RMD burden
  • QCD (Qualified Charitable Distribution): age 70½+, donate up to $105,000/year directly from IRA to charity — counts toward RMD but is excluded from income
Social Security Timing
  • Benefits can begin at 62 (reduced) or be deferred to 70 (maximum)
  • Each year of delay after FRA (66–67 depending on birth year) adds 8% per year
  • Up to 85% of Social Security benefits are includable in taxable income depending on combined income
  • Roth withdrawals and policy loans do not count toward the Social Security taxation threshold
Medicare IRMAA
  • High-income Medicare recipients pay income-related monthly adjustment amounts (IRMAA) on Part B and Part D premiums
  • Surcharges begin at approximately $103,000 single / $206,000 MFJ (2024 MAGI; 2-year look-back)
  • Large Roth conversions or asset sales can trigger IRMAA in future years
  • Planning to stay below IRMAA thresholds can save thousands per year in Medicare premiums
Non-Qualified Annuities

Unlike qualified plan annuities (fully taxable), non-qualified annuity payments use an exclusion ratio — only the earnings portion is taxable; the return of cost basis is tax-free. This can provide tax-efficient lifetime income.

Lesson 10: Transfer Planning

The transfer stage covers how wealth passes to heirs — and the US rules are fundamentally different from (and often more favorable than) those in Canada.

Step-Up in Basis at Death
No deemed disposition in the US. When a client dies, inherited assets receive a step-up in basis to fair market value at the date of death. All unrealized capital gains accumulated during the client's lifetime are permanently eliminated — heirs inherit the asset as if they paid today's market price for it.

Example: A client bought stock for $50,000 worth $400,000 at death. The heir's cost basis is $400,000 — if they sell immediately, they owe zero capital gains tax. This is a major transfer-planning tool.

Spousal Transfer
  • Assets can pass to a surviving US citizen spouse free of estate and gift tax (unlimited marital deduction)
  • Inherited IRA: a surviving spouse can roll the deceased's IRA into their own, deferring RMDs until age 73
  • Portability: a surviving spouse can claim the deceased's unused estate tax exemption
Federal Estate and Gift Tax
  • 2024 exemption: $13.61M per individual ($27.22M per married couple with portability)
  • Annual gift tax exclusion: $18,000 per recipient (2024)
  • Estates above the exemption: taxed at up to 40%
  • Note: The current exemption is scheduled to sunset after 2025 — roughly halving without Congressional action
Section 1202 QSBS Exclusion

Investors in qualifying small business stock (C-corporations) held more than 5 years may exclude up to 100% of capital gains (up to $10M or 10× basis). This is the US equivalent of Canada's Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption for small business shares.

Estate Attorney Referral

Estate planning — wills, trusts, beneficiary designations, powers of attorney — requires a licensed estate attorney. Always refer clients to qualified legal counsel for transfer-stage planning.

Lesson 11: Income Strategies for Married Clients

The US does not have a formal pension income splitting election like Canada. However, several strategies allow married clients to manage combined tax exposure effectively.

Married Filing Jointly (MFJ)
  • MFJ provides wider tax brackets than single filing — inherent income pooling advantage
  • Standard deduction: $29,200 MFJ (2024) vs. $14,600 single
  • Lower-earning spouse effectively shelters income at the household level
Spousal Roth Conversions
  • In years when income is lower (early retirement, partial year), convert Traditional IRA to Roth to fill up lower brackets
  • Both spouses' retirement income can be managed jointly to stay within target tax brackets
Social Security Coordination
  • A lower-earning spouse can claim spousal benefit (up to 50% of higher earner's FRA benefit)
  • Coordinating claiming ages can maximize lifetime household benefit — often delay higher earner to 70, claim lower earner earlier
IRMAA Bracket Management
  • Monitor MAGI to stay below IRMAA surcharge thresholds
  • Roth withdrawals and policy loans help manage MAGI
  • QCDs from IRA accounts reduce MAGI while satisfying RMDs for charitably-inclined clients
Charitable Strategies
  • Donor-Advised Fund (DAF): lump-sum contribution for immediate deduction, grants to charities over time
  • Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD): age 70½+, up to $105,000/year directly from IRA — excluded from income, counts toward RMD
  • Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT): provides income stream, then transfers to charity at death
Assignment of income doctrine: The US generally requires income to be taxed in the hands of the person who earned it — formal income splitting to family members (beyond spousal strategies) is limited. Proper structuring through beneficiary-designated accounts and trusts is essential.
